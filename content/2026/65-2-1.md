@topic Types of relations
A relation R on set $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$ defined as $R = \{(1, 1), (2, 2), (1, 2)\}$ is
(A) Reflexive only
(B) Reflexive and Transitive
(C) Symmetric and Transitive
(D) Transitive only
--- ans D
**Not reflexive:** $(3, 3) \notin R$ although $3 \in A$.
**Not symmetric:** $(1, 2) \in R$ but $(2, 1) \notin R$.
**Transitive:** the only chains are $(1, 1), (1, 2) \Rightarrow (1, 2) \in R$ and $(1, 2), (2, 2) \Rightarrow (1, 2) \in R$; no pair is missing.
So R is transitive only.

@topic Algebra of matrices
If A and B are square matrices of same order, then which of the following statements is/are always true?
(i) $(A + B)(A - B) = A^2 - B^2$
(ii) $AB = BA$
(iii) $(A + B)^2 = A^2 + AB + BA + B^2$
(iv) $AB = 0 \Rightarrow A = 0$ or $B = 0$
(A) Only (i) and (iii)
(B) Only (ii) and (iii)
(C) Only (iii)
(D) Only (iii) and (iv)
--- ans C
Matrix multiplication is not commutative, so (ii) is not always true, and
$(A + B)(A - B) = A^2 - AB + BA - B^2 \ne A^2 - B^2$ in general, so (i) fails.
$(A + B)^2 = (A + B)(A + B) = A^2 + AB + BA + B^2$ always — (iii) is **true**.
(iv) is false: e.g. $A = B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \ne O$ but $AB = O$.

=== 21a | rf | 2 | sub
@topic Onto functions
Check whether $f : \mathbb{R} - \{3\} \to \mathbb{R}$ defined as $f(x) = \dfrac{x - 2}{x - 3}$ is onto or not.
--- ans
Let $y \in \mathbb{R}$ (co-domain) and suppose $f(x) = y$ for some $x \in \mathbb{R} - \{3\}$:
$$\frac{x - 2}{x - 3} = y \Rightarrow x - 2 = xy - 3y \Rightarrow x(1 - y) = 2 - 3y \Rightarrow x = \frac{2 - 3y}{1 - y}$$
This is not defined for $y = 1$. Indeed, $f(x) = 1 \Rightarrow x - 2 = x - 3 \Rightarrow -2 = -3$, which is impossible.
So $1 \in \mathbb{R}$ (co-domain) has no pre-image in $\mathbb{R} - \{3\}$.
$\therefore f$ is **not onto**.

=== 23b | aod | 2 | sub
@topic Rate of change of quantities
If the volume of a solid hemisphere increases at a uniform rate, prove that its surface area varies inversely as its radius.
--- ans
Let $r$ be the radius of the solid hemisphere at time $t$.
Volume $V = \dfrac{2}{3}\pi r^3$, total surface area $S = 2\pi r^2 + \pi r^2 = 3\pi r^2$ (curved surface + flat base).
Volume increases at a uniform rate: $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = k$ (constant).
$$\frac{dV}{dt} = 2\pi r^2\frac{dr}{dt} = k \Rightarrow \frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{k}{2\pi r^2}$$
$$\frac{dS}{dt} = 6\pi r\frac{dr}{dt} = 6\pi r\cdot\frac{k}{2\pi r^2} = \frac{3k}{r}$$
$\therefore \dfrac{dS}{dt} \propto \dfrac{1}{r}$, i.e. the (rate of change of the) surface area varies inversely as the radius. Hence proved.
(The statement means the rate of change of the surface area; the surface area itself, $3\pi r^2$, grows with $r$.)
