If matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} -p & q \\ r & p \end{bmatrix}$ is such that $A^2 = I$, then :
(A) $1 + p^2 + qr = 0$
(B) $1 - p^2 - qr = 0$
(C) $1 - p^2 + qr = 0$
(D) $1 + p^2 - qr = 0$
--- ans B
$$A^2 = \begin{bmatrix} -p & q \\ r & p \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -p & q \\ r & p \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} p^2 + qr & -pq + pq \\ -pr + pr & qr + p^2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} p^2 + qr & 0 \\ 0 & p^2 + qr \end{bmatrix}$$
$A^2 = I \Rightarrow p^2 + qr = 1 \Rightarrow 1 - p^2 - qr = 0$.

@topic Equivalence relation
**Assertion (A):** A relation R on the set $\{1, 2, 3\}$ defined as $R = \{(1, 1), (1, 2), (2, 1), (2, 2), (3, 3)\}$ is an equivalence relation.
**Reason (R):** A relation that is reflexive, symmetric and transitive is an equivalence relation.
(A) Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true and Reason (R) is the correct explanation of the Assertion (A).
(B) Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true, but Reason (R) is ***not*** the correct explanation of the Assertion (A).
(C) Assertion (A) is true, but Reason (R) is false.
(D) Assertion (A) is false, but Reason (R) is true.
--- ans A
Reflexive: $(1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 3) \in R$. Symmetric: $(1, 2) \in R$ and $(2, 1) \in R$.
Transitive: $(1, 2), (2, 1) \Rightarrow (1, 1) \in R$; $(2, 1), (1, 2) \Rightarrow (2, 2) \in R$; all other pairs involve $(a, a)$.
So R is an equivalence relation — Assertion is **true**. Reason is the definition of an equivalence relation — **true**, and it is exactly what is used to verify A, so R correctly explains A.

**Assertion (A):** Consider a Linear Programming Problem with minimise $Z = x + 2y$ subject to constraints $2x + y \ge 3$, $x + 2y \ge 6$, $x, y \ge 0$ which gives minimum Z at infinitely many points. The corner points of feasible region are $(0, 3)$ and $(6, 0)$.
**Reason (R):** If two corner points produce the same minimum value of the objective function, then every point on the line segment joining the points will give the same minimum value.
(A) Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true and Reason (R) is the correct explanation of the Assertion (A).
(B) Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true, but Reason (R) is ***not*** the correct explanation of the Assertion (A).
(C) Assertion (A) is true, but Reason (R) is false.
(D) Assertion (A) is false, but Reason (R) is true.
--- ans A
The lines $2x + y = 3$ and $x + 2y = 6$ meet at $(0, 3)$; the unbounded feasible region has corner points $(0, 3)$ and $(6, 0)$.
$Z(0, 3) = 6$ and $Z(6, 0) = 6$. Since $x + 2y < 6$ has no point in common with the feasible region, the minimum is $6$, attained at every point of the segment joining $(0, 3)$ and $(6, 0)$ — Assertion is **true**.
Reason is a standard true result and it is exactly why the minimum occurs at infinitely many points, so R correctly explains A.
