@topic Operations on matrices
**Assertion (A):** If A and B are two square matrices such that AB and BA are defined, then it is not necessary that $AB = BA$.
**Reason (R):** Product of two diagonal matrices of same order is commutative.
(A) Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true and Reason (R) is the correct explanation of the Assertion (A).
(B) Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true, but Reason (R) is ***not*** the correct explanation of the Assertion (A).

@topic One-one and onto functions
**Assertion (A):** A function $f : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ given by $f(x) = x^3 + 2,\ \forall\, x \in \mathbb{N}$ is one-one but not onto.
**Reason (R):** Since $\forall\, y \in \mathbb{N}$ (Codomain), there does not exist $x = (y - 2)^{1/3}$ in $\mathbb{N}$ (Domain) such that $f(x) = x^3 + 2 = y$.
(A) Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true and Reason (R) is the correct explanation of the Assertion (A).
(B) Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true, but Reason (R) is ***not*** the correct explanation of the Assertion (A).

(D) Assertion (A) is false, but Reason (R) is true.

Show that the function $f(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\cos x}{-x + \frac{\pi}{2}}, & x \ne \dfrac{\pi}{2} \\[2mm] 1, & x = \dfrac{\pi}{2} \end{cases}$ is continuous at $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$.

=== 22b | cd | 2 | sub
@topic Differentiability
Find whether the function $f(x) = \begin{cases} x - 1, & x < 2 \\ 2x - 3, & x \ge 2 \end{cases}$ at $x = 2$ is differentiable or not.
--- ans
$f(2) = 2(2) - 3 = 1$.
$$\text{LHD} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(2 - h) - f(2)}{-h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(2 - h - 1) - 1}{-h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-h}{-h} = 1$$
$$\text{RHD} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(2 + h) - f(2)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(4 + 2h - 3) - 1}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2h}{h} = 2$$
Since LHD $\ne$ RHD, $f$ is **not differentiable at $x = 2$**.

@topic Increasing and decreasing functions
Find the values of x for which $f(x) = x^x,\ x > 0$ is increasing.
--- ans
Let $y = x^x \Rightarrow \log y = x\log x$. Differentiating, $\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \log x + 1$.
$$f'(x) = x^x(1 + \log x)$$
$f'(x) = 0 \Rightarrow 1 + \log x = 0 \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{1}{e}$ (as $x^x > 0$ for $x > 0$).
For $x > \dfrac{1}{e}$: $\log x > -1$, so $f'(x) > 0$. For $0 < x < \dfrac{1}{e}$: $f'(x) < 0$.
$\therefore f$ is increasing for $x \ge \dfrac{1}{e}$, i.e. on $\left[\dfrac{1}{e}, \infty\right)$.
