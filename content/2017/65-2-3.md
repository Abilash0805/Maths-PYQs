@topic Rate of change
The radius r of a right circular cylinder is increasing uniformly at the rate of 0.3 cm/s and its height h is decreasing at the rate of 0.4 cm/s. When $r = 3.5$ cm and $h = 7$ cm, find the rate of change of the curved surface area of the cylinder. [Use $\pi = \dfrac{22}{7}$]
--- ans
Given $\dfrac{dr}{dt} = 0.3$ cm/s and $\dfrac{dh}{dt} = -0.4$ cm/s.
Curved surface area $S = 2\pi rh$, so
$$\frac{dS}{dt} = 2\pi\left(h\frac{dr}{dt} + r\frac{dh}{dt}\right)$$
At $r = 3.5$, $h = 7$:
$$\frac{dS}{dt} = 2 \times \frac{22}{7}\Big[7(0.3) + 3.5(-0.4)\Big] = \frac{44}{7}(2.1 - 1.4) = \frac{44}{7} \times 0.7 = 4.4$$
$\therefore$ The curved surface area is **increasing** at the rate of $4.4\ \text{cm}^2/\text{s}$.

@topic Graphical method – bounded region
Solve the following LPP graphically :
Maximise $Z = 105x + 90y$
subject to the constraints
$x + y \le 50$
$2x + y \le 80$
$x \ge 0,\ y \ge 0.$
--- ans
Draw the lines $x + y = 50$ (through $(50, 0)$, $(0, 50)$) and $2x + y = 80$ (through $(40, 0)$, $(0, 80)$). Both constraints are satisfied on the origin side of the lines, in the first quadrant.

Intersection: $x + y = 50$ and $2x + y = 80$ give $x = 30$, $y = 20$, i.e. $B(30, 20)$.

```plot
{"x":[-5,60],"y":[-5,85],"lines":[{"a":1,"b":1,"c":50,"label":"x + y = 50"},{"a":2,"b":1,"c":80,"label":"2x + y = 80"}],"polygons":[{"pts":[[0,0],[40,0],[30,20],[0,50]],"label":"Feasible region"}],"points":[{"x":0,"y":0,"label":"O(0, 0)"},{"x":40,"y":0,"label":"A(40, 0)"},{"x":30,"y":20,"label":"B(30, 20)"},{"x":0,"y":50,"label":"C(0, 50)"}]}
```

The feasible region OABC is bounded.

| Corner point | $Z = 105x + 90y$ |
|---|---|
| $O(0, 0)$ | $0$ |
| $A(40, 0)$ | $4200$ |
| $B(30, 20)$ | $4950$ ← maximum |

$\therefore$ Maximum $Z = 4950$ at $x = 30,\ y = 20$.

@topic Homogeneous equations
Find the general solution of the differential equation
$x\cos\left(\dfrac{y}{x}\right)\dfrac{dy}{dx} = y\cos\left(\dfrac{y}{x}\right) + x.$
--- ans
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y\cos\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) + x}{x\cos\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)} = \frac{y}{x} + \frac{1}{\cos\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)}$$
This is homogeneous. Put $y = vx$, $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = v + x\dfrac{dv}{dx}$:
$$v + x\frac{dv}{dx} = v + \frac{1}{\cos v} \Rightarrow x\frac{dv}{dx} = \frac{1}{\cos v}$$
Separating the variables and integrating:
$$\int \cos v\,dv = \int \frac{dx}{x} \Rightarrow \sin v = \log|x| + C$$
$\therefore$ The general solution is
$$\sin\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) = \log|x| + C$$

=== 22 | itf | 4 | sub
@topic Simplification
Prove that :
$$\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{1 + x^2} + \sqrt{1 - x^2}}{\sqrt{1 + x^2} - \sqrt{1 - x^2}}\right) = \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{1}{2}\cos^{-1}x^2;\quad -1 < x < 1$$
--- ans
Put $x^2 = \cos 2\theta$, i.e. $\theta = \dfrac{1}{2}\cos^{-1}x^2$. For $x \ne 0$, $0 < x^2 < 1$, so $0 < 2\theta < \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, i.e. $0 < \theta < \dfrac{\pi}{4}$, and $\sin\theta, \cos\theta > 0$.
$$\sqrt{1 + x^2} = \sqrt{1 + \cos 2\theta} = \sqrt{2}\cos\theta, \qquad \sqrt{1 - x^2} = \sqrt{1 - \cos 2\theta} = \sqrt{2}\sin\theta$$
$$\text{LHS} = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\cos\theta + \sqrt{2}\sin\theta}{\sqrt{2}\cos\theta - \sqrt{2}\sin\theta}\right) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1 + \tan\theta}{1 - \tan\theta}\right) = \tan^{-1}\left[\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{4} + \theta\right)\right]$$
Since $\dfrac{\pi}{4} < \dfrac{\pi}{4} + \theta < \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, this equals $\dfrac{\pi}{4} + \theta$.
$$\therefore\ \text{LHS} = \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{1}{2}\cos^{-1}x^2 = \text{RHS}$$
Hence proved. (At $x = 0$ the denominator on the left vanishes; the identity then holds in the limiting sense, both sides tending to $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$.)

@topic Systems of linear equations
If $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, find $A^{-1}$ and hence solve the system of equations $x - 2y = 10$, $2x + y + 3z = 8$ and $-2y + z = 7$.
--- ans
$$|A| = 1(1 + 6) - (-2)(2 - 0) + 0 = 7 + 4 = 11 \ne 0$$
so $A^{-1}$ exists. Cofactors:
$A_{11} = 7,\ A_{12} = -2,\ A_{13} = -4$
$A_{21} = 2,\ A_{22} = 1,\ A_{23} = 2$
$A_{31} = -6,\ A_{32} = -3,\ A_{33} = 5$
$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{|A|}\operatorname{adj}A = \frac{1}{11}\begin{bmatrix} 7 & 2 & -6 \\ -2 & 1 & -3 \\ -4 & 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$$
The given system is $AX = B$, where $X = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 8 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}$.
$$X = A^{-1}B = \frac{1}{11}\begin{bmatrix} 70 + 16 - 42 \\ -20 + 8 - 21 \\ -40 + 16 + 35 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{11}\begin{bmatrix} 44 \\ -33 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
$\therefore\ \boxed{x = 4,\ y = -3,\ z = 1}$

=== 29 | de | 6 | sub
@topic Linear differential equations
Find the particular solution of the differential equation
$(1 + y^2) + \left(x - e^{\tan^{-1}y}\right)\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 0$, given that $y = 0$ when $x = 1$.
--- ans
The equation can be written as
$$\frac{dx}{dy} = -\frac{x - e^{\tan^{-1}y}}{1 + y^2} \Rightarrow \frac{dx}{dy} + \frac{1}{1 + y^2}\,x = \frac{e^{\tan^{-1}y}}{1 + y^2}$$
This is linear in $x$ with $P = \dfrac{1}{1 + y^2}$, $Q = \dfrac{e^{\tan^{-1}y}}{1 + y^2}$.
I.F. $= e^{\int \frac{dy}{1 + y^2}} = e^{\tan^{-1}y}$.
Solution:
$$x\,e^{\tan^{-1}y} = \int \frac{e^{2\tan^{-1}y}}{1 + y^2}\,dy$$
Put $\tan^{-1}y = t \Rightarrow \dfrac{dy}{1 + y^2} = dt$:
$$x\,e^{\tan^{-1}y} = \int e^{2t}\,dt = \frac{e^{2t}}{2} + C = \frac{1}{2}e^{2\tan^{-1}y} + C$$
When $y = 0$, $x = 1$: $1 \cdot e^0 = \dfrac{1}{2} + C \Rightarrow C = \dfrac{1}{2}$.
$\therefore$ The particular solution is
$$x\,e^{\tan^{-1}y} = \frac{1}{2}e^{2\tan^{-1}y} + \frac{1}{2}, \quad\text{i.e.}\quad 2x\,e^{\tan^{-1}y} = e^{2\tan^{-1}y} + 1$$
